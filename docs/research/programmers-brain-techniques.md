# Cognitive-Science Technique Catalog: *The Programmer's Brain*

This is a reference catalog (not a narrative) of the eight cognitive-science techniques described or drawn upon in Felienne Hermans' *The Programmer's Brain* (Manning, 2021), written to inform the session-mechanics and scheduling design of a subject-agnostic learning app. Each section states the claim, a subject-agnostic operationalization, and any cadence/dosage evidence. Citations follow the claim back to its actual owner; sources marked **(secondary)** were not verified against Hermans' own words or the cited primary paper — see the Gaps section at the end.

**Access note:** The book's full text was not directly accessible. Manning's liveBook, O'Reilly's hosted edition, and two independent PDF mirrors (scispace, leadinglearner.me for an unrelated Sweller paper) either 403'd or returned only a partial table of contents. Claims attributed to "the book" below come from (a) Hermans' own conference talk (transcribed directly via `yt-dlp` from [Lambda Days 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K9k1XM_GjoM)), which covers much of the same material as the book in her own words, and (b) detailed third-party chapter notes/reviews, marked secondary. Numbers attributed to "the book" via secondary sources should be treated as unverified until checked against the actual text.

## 1. Working memory limits

**1. Claim/mechanism.** Human information processing is constrained by a capacity- and duration-limited working memory. Three distinct numbers circulate in the literature, each with a different owner and referent:
- George Miller (1956), "The Magical Number Seven, Plus or Minus Two" — the classic estimate for immediate/short-term memory span is **7±2** items. Hermans cites this figure by name in her own talk: *"your short-term memory... since the 1950s this was discovered by George Miller... your short-term memory can only hold between five and nine things at the same time"* (Hermans, [Lambda Days 2022 keynote](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K9k1XM_GjoM), transcript captured via `yt-dlp`).
- Peterson & Peterson (1959) established the **duration** limits of working memory (rapid decay of unrehearsed information), cited alongside Miller in Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, ["Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later,"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5) *Educational Psychology Review* (2019) — verified by direct PDF read: *"The capacity and duration limits of working memory have been known at least since Miller (1956) and Peterson and Peterson (1959)."*
- Nelson Cowan (2001), ["The magical number 4 in short-term memory: A reconsideration of mental storage capacity,"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/44023F1147D4A1D44BDC0AD226838496/S0140525X01003922a.pdf/the-magical-number-4-in-short-term-memory-a-reconsideration-of-mental-storage-capacity.pdf) *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* — argues the true capacity limit of the *focus of attention* within working memory is closer to **~4 chunks**, not 7.
- Hermans' own book reportedly settles on an estimate of **2-6 chunks** of working-memory capacity, reflecting the tighter Cowan-era consensus **(secondary** — reported consistently across [understandlegacycode.com](https://understandlegacycode.com/blog/key-points-of-programmer-brain/), a [GitBook chapter-notes page](https://yoan-thirion.gitbook.io/knowledge-base/software-craftsmanship/the-programmers-brain), and a [GitHub chapter-notes repo](https://github.com/visualpartnership/the-programmers-brain), but not confirmed against Hermans' own text).
- Decay: unrehearsed information is lost from working memory within roughly **30 seconds** if not processed further **(secondary,** [understandlegacycode.com](https://understandlegacycode.com/blog/key-points-of-programmer-brain/)).

**2. Operationalization.** In a subject-agnostic app, this caps how much *new, unfamiliar* material should be presented in a single working step, regardless of subject: a vocabulary drill should surface no more than ~4 new words per micro-session; a math derivation should be broken so no single screen requires holding more than ~4 unfamiliar intermediate quantities at once; a history timeline exercise should chunk events into groups of ~4 before asking learners to connect them. The generalizable mechanic is a **hard cap on simultaneous novel elements per exercise step**, enforced by the app's content/exercise generator, not just a programming-specific "how many variables are on screen" heuristic.

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions.** No single number is uncontested — treat "3-5 novel items per step" as the actionable range (bounded below by Cowan's ~4 and above by Miller's 7±2), and treat decay as meaning **working memory content not acted upon within ~30 seconds should be assumed lost**, which argues for keeping any single working-memory-dependent exercise step under roughly that duration before requiring a response or an externalized aid (e.g., a scratchpad/state table — see Notional Machines below). This threshold should be flagged for verification against the book's actual text before being hard-coded into scheduling logic.

## 2. Long-term memory (LTM) vs short-term memory (STM), and information transfer between them

**1. Claim/mechanism.** Cognitive load theory's architecture (as restated by Sweller himself) holds that novel information is first processed by capacity/duration-limited working memory and then, if consolidated, is stored in an effectively unlimited long-term memory; once information resides in LTM its capacity/duration limits "disappear," transforming the learner's ability to process related information going forward (Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5), verified by direct read: *"cognitive load theory emphasised that all novel information is first processed by a capacity and duration limited working memory and then stored in an unlimited long-term memory for later use. Once information is stored in long-term memory, the capacity and duration limits of working memory disappear."*). This paper also cites Ericsson & Kintsch's (1995) theory of *long-term working memory* and De Groot's (1946/1965) chess-expertise findings as the empirical basis: expert chess players' skill was fully explained by memorized board configurations held in LTM, not by superior raw working-memory capacity.
Hermans' own talk restates the pipeline in her own words: information first lands in STM ("stored... briefly"), and is then handed to working memory, which "collaborates with your long-term memory" — LTM supplies related prior knowledge that helps working memory interpret the new input (Hermans, [Lambda Days 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K9k1XM_GjoM) transcript). The book structures this as three cognitive processes relevant to confusion: lack of knowledge (LTM), lack of information (STM), lack of processing power (working memory) — this chapter structure is confirmed directly from the [O'Reilly-hosted table of contents](https://livebook.manning.com/book/the-programmers-brain/contents/) (Chapter 1: "Decoding your confusion while coding," sections "LTM and programming," "STM and programming," "Working memory and programming").

**2. Operationalization.** The generalizable mechanic: a learning app should explicitly separate "things the learner should recognize instantly because they're in LTM" (facts, vocabulary, formulas, dates) from "things assembled in the moment using working memory" (applying a rule, translating a sentence, solving a problem). Practice should be structured to migrate items from slow/effortful (STM/working-memory-bound, requires external support) to fast/automatic (LTM-resident, retrievable without support) — e.g., a language app drilling verb conjugations until they're recalled without conscious rule-application, or a math app drilling multiplication facts until they stop being computed and start being recalled.

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions.** No specific interval is given for the STM-to-LTM transfer itself in the sources found; the transfer is described as a function of rehearsal/retrieval practice (see Technique 8) rather than a fixed timer. The actionable design implication is structural, not temporal: the app should track, per learning item, whether it is still "working-memory-bound" (slow, effortful, error-prone) or has become "LTM-resident" (fast, low-error) and adjust support scaffolding accordingly — this maps onto the storage-strength/retrieval-strength distinction discussed in Technique 8.

## 3. Chunking

**1. Claim/mechanism.** Experts do not process a domain's raw elements one at a time; they group familiar combinations into single "chunks" that occupy one working-memory slot each, allowing far more effective information to be held and manipulated than a novice with the same working-memory capacity. The canonical grounding is De Groot's chess-expertise research (1946/1965, cited in Sweller et al. [2019](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5)): expert chess skill was explained by memorized configurations, not superior raw capacity. Hermans' talk gives a direct, code-specific instance of the same mechanism: rewriting unfamiliar code into a more familiar form makes it "less chunked" as letter-soup and more organized/parseable — i.e., familiarity converts many small elements into fewer, larger chunks (Hermans, [Lambda Days 2022](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K9k1XM_GjoM) transcript: *"rewriting this code in a more familiar form means that for you it's less chunked... you move it from being this letter soup towards actually being organiz[ed]"*). Secondary chapter notes attribute a specific study (McKeithen et al.) to the book, contrasting expert vs. novice programmers' recall of structured vs. randomized code, mirroring De Groot's chess design **(secondary,** [GitBook notes](https://yoan-thirion.gitbook.io/knowledge-base/software-craftsmanship/the-programmers-brain), not independently verified).

**2. Operationalization.** Subject-agnostic mechanic: teach and drill the mid-level "chunks" of a domain explicitly, not just atomic facts or whole procedures — e.g., common musical chord progressions (not individual notes) in music theory, common grammatical constructions (not individual words) in language learning, common proof strategies (not individual algebraic steps) in math, or recognizable narrative/causal patterns (not individual dates) in history. The app should have a concept of "named chunks" per subject domain, and track a learner's growing library of recognized chunks as a distinct progression axis from raw fact recall.

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions.** No dosage number was found beyond the general point that chunk-recognition is built through repeated exposure to the same structural pattern across varied surface content — an argument for **interleaving** varied examples of the same underlying chunk (e.g., many different code snippets that are all "loop with an accumulator," or many different sentences that are all "past-perfect construction") rather than drilling one example repeatedly. This is consistent with, but not the same claim as, spaced repetition (Technique 8).

## 4. Notional machines (mental models of program execution)

**1. Claim/mechanism.** A notional machine is a pedagogic device — a simplified, idealized model of how the underlying system executes — that helps a learner reason about behavior without needing the full real complexity. The term originates with Du Boulay, O'Shea & Monk (1981), "The black box inside the glass box: presenting computing concepts to novices," and is developed further in du Boulay (1986), ["Some Difficulties of Learning to Program,"](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.2190/3LFX-9RRF-67T8-UVK9) *Journal of Educational Computing Research* — found via search-result summary; primary text not directly fetched, so treat the exact wording as unverified. Hermans is a named co-author on the field's most recent synthesis, Fincher, Jeuring, Miller, Donaldson, du Boulay, Hauswirth, Hellas, Hermans, Lewis, Mühling, Pearce & Petersen, ["Notional Machines in Computing Education: The Education of Attention,"](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3437800.3439202) ITiCSE-WGR '20. Hermans' own blog post, ["Forms of notional machines,"](https://www.felienne.com/archives/6392) gives her working definition directly and primarily: *"a didactical tool that helps a learner understand the workings of the source code on an actual machine."* She distinguishes four forms notional machines can take — **metaphors** (e.g., her "box" model for variables, refined into "sticky notes" to avoid implying a variable holds multiple values at once), **representations** (tools like PythonTutor that depict actual memory state with less translation loss but more visual load), **rules** (precise logical equivalences, e.g., explaining a `for` loop via an equivalent `while` loop, at the cost of abstractness), and **actions** (habitual practices like always drawing a diagram, credited to Katie Cunningham) — and explicitly flags as an open question how these forms should be combined or sequenced in a curriculum.

**2. Operationalization.** This generalizes directly beyond programming: any domain with a "hidden mechanism" a learner must reason about benefits from an explicit, simplified model of that mechanism — a mental model of how digestion works for a biology app, a simplified model of supply-and-demand curves for economics, a "carrying the one" mental procedure for arithmetic, or a metaphorical model of grammatical case for a language with declensions. The mechanic for the app: for each subject/skill, define and explicitly teach one canonical simplified execution/reasoning model (pick one of Hermans' four forms deliberately — metaphor, representation, rule, or action) before asking learners to apply it, and provide a consistent visual/interactive representation of that model across exercises rather than switching models arbitrarily.

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions.** No cadence or dosage evidence was found — this technique is about *what* mental model to teach and *how* to represent it, not *how often*. Hermans explicitly notes the open question of whether curricula should hold one notional-machine form constant or deliberately vary forms; this is a design choice, not an evidenced interval, and should not be treated as informing session-scheduling cadence.

## 5. Roles of variables (Sajaniemi's taxonomy)

**1. Claim/mechanism.** Jorma Sajaniemi's research establishes that variables in procedural programs play a small set of recurring "roles" — stereotyped usage patterns — and that recognizing these roles supports schema formation for novice programmers. Fetched directly from Sajaniemi's own site, ["An introduction to the roles of variables"](https://saja.kapsi.fi/var_roles/role_intro.html) (primary source), the eleven roles and their informal definitions are:

| Role | Definition (Sajaniemi's own wording) |
|---|---|
| Fixed value | A data item that does not get a new proper value after its initialization |
| Stepper | A data item stepping through a systematic, predictable succession of values |
| Most-recent holder | Holds the latest value encountered in going through a succession of unpredictable values |
| Most-wanted holder | Holds the best or otherwise most appropriate value encountered so far |
| Gatherer | Accumulates the effect of individual values |
| Follower | Gets its new value always from the old value of some other data item |
| One-way flag | A two-valued data item that cannot get its initial value once changed |
| Temporary | Holds some value for a very short time only |
| Organizer | A data structure storing elements that can be rearranged |
| Container | A data structure storing elements that can be added and removed |
| Walker | A data item traversing a data structure |

The underlying empirical claim — that a small role vocabulary covers the overwhelming majority of variable usage in novice code — traces to Sajaniemi, "An empirical analysis of roles of variables in novice-level procedural programs," *Proceedings of IEEE 2002 Symposia on Human-Centric Computing Languages and Environments (HCC'02)*, pp. 37-39 (citation found via search-result summary quoting the paper's own claim of ~99% coverage; the primary PDF could not be rendered — a fetch attempt on the related [2005 PPIG paper](https://www.ppig.org/files/2005-PPIG-17th-sajaniemi.pdf) returned only binary/unreadable content, so the exact 99% figure is unverified against primary text and should be treated as a search-summary claim, not a confirmed quote). Hermans' book reportedly restates this taxonomy closely (naming "flag" rather than "one-way flag," otherwise matching) as a tool for building code-reading schemas **(secondary,** consistent across a [book review](https://ianhopkinson.org.uk/2022/02/book-review-the-programmers-brain-by-felienne-hermans/) and the [GitBook notes](https://yoan-thirion.gitbook.io/knowledge-base/software-craftsmanship/the-programmers-brain); Hermans' own restatement not directly fetched).

**2. Operationalization.** The generalizable mechanic is **role-tagging of atomic elements within a larger structure**, not variables specifically: in language learning, tag words in a sentence by grammatical/semantic role (subject, modifier, connector); in math, tag terms in an equation by role (the unknown, a fixed constant, an accumulator in a running sum); in history, tag entities in a causal narrative by role (a triggering event, a recurring actor, a turning point). The app can generalize Sajaniemi's insight as: **any domain has a small, learnable vocabulary of structural roles that recur across surface-different examples, and explicitly naming/tagging those roles accelerates comprehension** — this becomes a cross-subject exercise type (given an artifact, correctly label the role each part plays), not a programming-only feature.

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions.** No cadence evidence — this is a content-classification technique, not a scheduling one. The relevant design fact is quantitative coverage, not frequency: Sajaniemi's claim (per search summary, unverified) that ~10-11 roles cover ~99% of cases in novice code suggests that for any subject, the app's role-taxonomy for a given skill should aim to be similarly small (single digits to low teens) to remain learnable, rather than sprawling.

## 6. Cognitive load types: intrinsic, extraneous, germane

**1. Claim/mechanism.** This is the best-sourced technique in this catalog: verified by directly reading pages 1-4 of Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, ["Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later,"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09465-5) *Educational Psychology Review* (2019) (PDF fetched and read directly, not via search summary). This paper is itself the 20-years-later retrospective by the original authors of Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, "Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design," *Educational Psychology Review*, 10, 251-296 (1998) — the paper Hermans' book draws its three-part framework from. Roots trace further back to Sweller & Levine (1982) and the first full description in Sweller (1988), "Cognitive Load During Problem Solving: Effects on Learning," *Cognitive Science*. A related, independently findable primary paper is Chandler & Sweller (1991), "Cognitive Load Theory and the Format of Instruction," *Cognition and Instruction*, 8, 293-332 (citation confirmed via multiple indexes; full text not directly fetched — 403'd — so its wording is not directly quoted here).

Exact definitions, quoted directly from the 2019 primary source:
- **Intrinsic cognitive load** "referred to the complexity of the information being processed and was related to the concept of element interactivity... Intrinsic cognitive load is determined by both the complexity of the information and the knowledge of the person processing that information... intrinsic cognitive load only can be changed by changing what needs to be learned or changing the expertise of the learner."
- **Extraneous cognitive load** "is not determined by the intrinsic complexity of the information but rather, how the information is presented and what the learner is required to do... it can be changed by changing instructional procedures."
- **Germane cognitive load** "was defined as the cognitive load required to learn, which refers to the working memory resources that are devoted to dealing with intrinsic cognitive load rather than extraneous cognitive load." The paper explicitly flags that this is a **revision** from the 1998 formulation, where germane load was treated as additive to total load; the 2019 view treats it as **redistributive** — germane load is working-memory capacity freed from extraneous processing and redirected at intrinsic processing, not a separate load source.

Hermans' book reportedly applies this three-way split directly to code reading: intrinsic load = inherent problem complexity (unchangeable without changing the problem); extraneous load = accidental complexity from how code is written/presented (unfamiliar syntax, poor naming — changeable via refactoring or familiarity); germane load = the effort of actually storing new understanding in LTM **(secondary,** [GitBook notes](https://yoan-thirion.gitbook.io/knowledge-base/software-craftsmanship/the-programmers-brain); book's own wording not directly fetched).

**2. Operationalization.** This maps cleanly onto any subject: intrinsic load is set by the inherent difficulty of the target skill relative to the learner's current expertise (app lever: sequence content by genuine conceptual difficulty, not just by surface length); extraneous load is caused by *how* the material is presented — cluttered UI, inconsistent notation, unfamiliar terminology used before it's taught (app lever: strip presentation-caused friction — consistent formatting, pre-teach notation, remove distracting UI during a drill); germane load is the "good" effort of actively building a schema (app lever: prompt self-explanation, worked-example-then-fade sequencing, retrieval practice — anything that spends freed-up working memory on integrating the new material rather than decoding its presentation). Concretely: a history-dates app should not bury which century something happened in (extraneous), should sequence easy-before-hard causal chains (intrinsic), and should ask learners to explain *why* an event mattered, not just recall that it happened (germane).

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions.** No temporal cadence is given — cognitive load theory is a load-management framework for a single learning episode's design, not a scheduling-interval theory. Its actionable implication for a scheduler is structural, not temporal: within any one exercise, total load (intrinsic + extraneous, since germane is now understood as redistributive rather than additive) must stay within the learner's working-memory budget (see Technique 1's ~3-5 novel-element range), which argues for **reducing extraneous load first** (fix presentation) before increasing intrinsic difficulty (introduce harder content), rather than doing both simultaneously.

## 7. Misconception patterns

**1. Claim/mechanism.** This is a Hermans-owned empirical result, directly attributable: Swidan, Hermans & Smit, ["Programming Misconceptions for School Students,"](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/programming-misconceptions-for-school-students/) *Proceedings of the 2018 ACM Conference on International Computing Education Research (ICER '18)* (citation and abstract summary confirmed via TU Delft's research portal; full paper text not directly fetched, so findings below are from the portal's abstract summary, not the paper itself). The study tested 145 students aged 7-17 on 11 established programming misconceptions using Scratch-based multiple-choice exercises. Reported top patterns: difficulty grasping **sequential execution** of statements; the misconception that **"a variable holds one value at a time"** (as opposed to correctly modeling reassignment); confusion about **program interactivity/responsiveness** to input. The study also found misconceptions correlated with mathematical properties of numbers used, the semantic suggestiveness of variable names, and inflated expectations of what a computer "understands." A distinct secondary-sourced claim about the book's own framing: misconceptions are confidently-held *incorrect* mental models (not mere gaps in knowledge), often formed via **negative transfer** — prior knowledge (e.g., from another programming language, or from natural-language semantics) actively interferes with correctly learning the new concept; simply telling a learner they are wrong does not fix a misconception, a replacement correct model must be built, and learners can relapse to the old misconception even after correctly learning the new one **(secondary,** [GitBook notes](https://yoan-thirion.gitbook.io/knowledge-base/software-craftsmanship/the-programmers-brain); not directly confirmed against Hermans' own text).

**2. Operationalization.** This generalizes as a content-authoring requirement, not a UI feature: for each skill in any subject, the app's content model should maintain a **catalog of known common misconceptions** (analogous to the 11 tested in Swidan/Hermans/Smit) rather than only correct-answer content — e.g., in language learning, the false-friend/negative-transfer error of assuming a cognate means the same thing as in the learner's native language; in math, the misconception that multiplication always makes numbers bigger; in history, conflating correlation-in-time with causation. Diagnostic exercises should be specifically designed to *elicit* the misconception (distinguishing "doesn't know" from "confidently believes the wrong thing"), and remediation content should explicitly present the correct model as a *replacement*, not just "you got that wrong" feedback — this is a distinct exercise type from ordinary practice.

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions.** No cadence evidence was found. The one dosage-adjacent finding is that misconception correction is not "one and done" — the source notes the risk of relapse to the old misconception even after apparently correct learning, which argues for the app **re-testing previously "corrected" misconceptions later** (as a distinct check within the spaced-review system in Technique 8) rather than treating a single correct answer as proof the misconception is resolved. This is inference from the secondary source, not an evidenced interval, and needs verification.

## 8. Spaced repetition, desirable difficulty, and retrieval-practice research

**1. Claim/mechanism.** The book reportedly builds its syntax-memorization advice (flashcards) on the classic forgetting-curve finding of Hermann Ebbinghaus (1885) — that forgetting is fastest immediately after learning and slows over time — and on the general finding that **actively retrieving** a memory strengthens it more than passive re-exposure **(secondary** for the Ebbinghaus attribution — reported via a [Medium book-notes post](https://medium.com/@zolletta/the-programmers-brain-4a670adf9bc0); not directly confirmed in Hermans' own words, though she does discuss "storage strength" vs. "retrieval strength" of memories in her own talk's Q&A on flashcards). The storage-strength/retrieval-strength distinction (frequency vs. ease of recall as two separable memory properties) is the hallmark terminology of Bjork & Bjork's "New Theory of Disuse" — this is a plausible underlying source for that part of the book's framework, but this is an inference on our part, not a confirmed citation; it was not independently verified that Hermans cites Bjork by name.

**2. Operationalization.** This is the most directly reusable technique for a subject-agnostic scheduler, since it's already domain-independent in the source material: any discrete, recallable fact or skill (a vocabulary word, a historical date, a math identity, a piece of code syntax) should be tracked as a flashcard-like spaced-repetition item, reviewed via **active retrieval** (recall-then-check, not re-reading) rather than passive review, with items that are answered correctly pushed to longer intervals and items answered incorrectly reset to shorter intervals — this is a direct description of a Leitner-style or SM2-style spaced-repetition algorithm, which the app should implement as its core review-scheduling engine regardless of subject.

**3. Evidence on cadence/conditions — flag for the scheduling ticket.** Several concrete numbers were reported, all via secondary sources and **not yet verified against the book's primary text**:
- **"After 2 days, just 25% of the knowledge remains in your LTM"** — a specific forgetting-curve figure attributed to the book **(secondary,** [GitBook notes](https://yoan-thirion.gitbook.io/knowledge-base/software-craftsmanship/the-programmers-brain), unverified).
- **Revisit flashcard sets roughly monthly** once mastered, as a maintenance cadence **(secondary,** same source, unverified).
- **~10 minutes/day** of flashcard review as a recommended practice dosage, with correct recall producing increasing intervals ("within a few weeks" to consolidate new information into durable memory) **(secondary,** [understandlegacycode.com](https://understandlegacycode.com/blog/key-points-of-programmer-brain/), unverified).
- Hermans' own talk (primary) discusses flashcards only qualitatively in Q&A — that they need to be actively guided/assigned rather than left to self-motivation in a classroom setting, and that some learners will engage with them more than others — but gives **no specific interval numbers** in the portion transcribed.
None of these specific numbers (2-day/25%, monthly maintenance, 10 min/day) should be hard-coded into the app's scheduler without confirming them against the actual book text or an underlying primary spaced-repetition source (e.g., Cepeda et al.'s spacing-effect meta-analyses, or Bjork's own work) — they are currently only as reliable as the secondary blog posts reporting them.

## Gaps / things a primary read of the book would resolve

- **The book's own text was never directly accessible.** Manning liveBook, O'Reilly's hosted edition, and dokumen.pub's MEAP preview all either 403'd or returned no usable content. Every claim marked "(secondary)" above is a third-party paraphrase of the book, not a confirmed quote, and should be checked against the physical/purchased text before being treated as authoritative for the app's design.
- **Working memory capacity figure for the book specifically** (the "2-6 chunks" number) is reported consistently across three independent secondary sources but was never seen in Hermans' own words — worth confirming, since it's the number most likely to anchor an exercise's "max novel elements" design parameter.
- **The 2-day/25% LTM-retention figure and the monthly flashcard-revisit rule** are the two most schedule-relevant numbers in this whole catalog and are the least verified — both come from a single detailed secondary source (the GitBook chapter notes) and were not corroborated by a second independent reading of the book. These should not be built into the review-scheduling ticket without primary confirmation.
- **Sajaniemi's ~99%-coverage claim** for the roles-of-variables taxonomy was only seen via a WebSearch-generated summary of the 2002 HCC paper, not the paper's own text (the linked PDF rendered as unreadable binary on fetch). The role *definitions* themselves, by contrast, were fetched directly from Sajaniemi's own site and are reliable.
- **Chandler & Sweller (1991)'s exact wording** was not obtained (403 on every attempted host). The intrinsic/extraneous/germane definitions used in this catalog instead come from the *2019* Sweller/van Merriënboer/Paas retrospective, fetched and read directly — this is a legitimate primary source (same lead author, direct quotation of definitions) but is a later restatement, not the original 1991/1998 wording, and the 2019 paper explicitly notes the germane-load definition changed between 1998 and 2019. If the book was written against the 1998 (additive) formulation rather than the 2019 (redistributive) one, the operationalization in Technique 6 may need adjusting.
- **Du Boulay's origin of "notional machine"** (1986, and the earlier 1981 paper with O'Shea & Monk) was reported via a WebSearch summary of secondary academic sources, not fetched from the original journal article — the exact definition quoted ("the idealised model of the computer implied by the constructs of the programming language") should be verified against the primary du Boulay text.
- **Whether Hermans cites Bjork by name** for the storage-strength/retrieval-strength distinction is an open question — this catalog treats it as a plausible but unconfirmed inference, not a citation.
- **The Swidan/Hermans/Smit (2018) misconceptions paper** was read only via the TU Delft research-portal's abstract summary, not the full ICER paper — the complete list of all 11 tested misconceptions (only the top 2-3 were reported in the summary) would be valuable to pull from the full text for building the app's misconception-catalog content.
- **Hermans' Lambda Days 2022 talk transcript** (the most primary source used in this catalog, since it's her own spoken words) is an auto-generated YouTube transcript, not a professionally verified one — minor transcription errors are possible, though the specific quotes used above (Miller citation, STM/working-memory/LTM pipeline, chunking example, flashcard Q&A) read as clean and internally consistent.
- **No chapter-and-verse mapping** was established between this catalog's 8 techniques and the book's actual chapter numbers/titles beyond Chapter 1 (confirmed via the partial livebook TOC) and the flashcards material (confirmed to be Chapter 3, "How to learn programming syntax more quickly," via search-result summary of the O'Reilly TOC, itself not directly fetched). A full chapter map would help the later session-mechanics ticket sequence content the way the book does.
